# Round 13: Categories forming from top-tier seed and Series A rounds (Jul 1 – Oct 5, 2026)

**Method.** I ran 40 web searches (the cap) across TechCrunch, Fortune Term Sheet, Axios Pro Rata, TheSaaSNews, Tech Startups daily roundups, dealroom, firm and company pages. WebFetch was blocked, so every row comes from search-result snippets. Nothing here is invented. Where a lead, date or category could not be confirmed, the row says so.

**Coverage, stated plainly.** The searches surfaced 55 rounds. **About 24 of them are B2B seed or Series A rounds led or co-led by a firm on the target list and announced inside the window.** That is short of the 40 target. The rest of the rows are context: rounds just outside the window, rounds led by firms not on the list, or excluded sectors. Search snippets under-report seed rounds, so the true count is higher.

**Main finding.** In this window, top-tier seed and A money went overwhelmingly into categories that are already crowded or that we have already killed (A, B, C, D). Only three rounds were the first meaningfully funded player in their category:
1. **Arrakis**: an operating system for deploying agents in industrial operations. It sells mainly in Europe; CVector is a small US player; Cognite and the process-software incumbents sit next to it.
2. **Ando**: team messaging built for agents.
3. **Corma**: a frontier model for cyber defense.

This repeats structural conclusion #1 in STATUS.md: gaps visible in public sources get funded within about six months, and by the time a round reaches the newsletters the category already has 3–10 players.

## 3. Top 3 entry proposals for a new team

The scores use the scoreboard's 1–10 scale. The bar is an average of 8.5 or more with no score below 7. **None of the three clears it.** They are ranked below by average score.

### #1. AI shift engineer for North American process plants (an angle on cluster E; first mover is Arrakis)

- **Pitch:** An AI agent that reads a process plant's historian, alarms, maintenance system and operator logs. It writes the shift handover and investigates every production deviation before the morning meeting.
- **Buyer:** Plant manager or VP of operations at mid-size continuous-process plants: chemicals, metals, cement, pulp and paper, food and beverage processing. Secondary buyer: the central operational-excellence team at multi-site operators.
- **Buyers × ACV (estimates, unverified):** About 8–12K US and Canadian process plants with more than 100 staff. Pricing is $60–120K per site per year.
  - $10M ARR ≈ 110 sites, about 20 multi-site customers.
  - $100M ARR ≈ 1,100 sites, about 10% of the market.
- **Why now:**
  - Industrial companies now have budget for agents. Arrakis raised $38M in six months on that premise.
  - Experienced operators are retiring.
  - LLMs can now read messy operator logs and historian tags together.
- **Funded first mover and room for a second:** Arrakis (Accel seed) is horizontal across many industries and sells mainly in Europe. CVector ($5M) focuses on margin optimization. The room is a specific workflow (handover and deviation investigation) for one segment in North America. **But** Seeq, AspenTech, AVEVA, Cognite, Palantir AIP and TwinThread all reach these buyers and are adding copilots. A second entrant is viable only if the workflow wedge is very sharp.
- **30–90 day pilot:** One plant, read-only access to the historian and maintenance system, 60 days. Pass if (a) 80% of handovers are AI-drafted and accepted, and (b) at least 3 deviations get a root cause the team had missed.
- **Moat:** A library of failure modes and fixes per unit operation, learned across sites, plus connectors to on-prem historians (OSIsoft PI, Honeywell PHD). Switching costs rise once the agent becomes part of daily operating meetings.
- **Kill risk:** The historian vendors (AVEVA/PI, AspenTech) ship this natively. IT/OT security reviews push pilots past 90 days. The sale needs on-site work that looks like the killed thesis R3.
- **Scores:** Pain 7 · Urgency 6 · ROI clarity 6 · Customer accessibility 5 · Pilot speed 5 · Market size 7 · Expansion 7 · Venture potential 7 · Defensibility 6 · Why now 7 · Competition position 5 → **avg 6.2**

### #2. "Purple team" range that tests a SOC against autonomous AI attackers (an angle on Corma)

- **Pitch:** Continuous AI-vs-AI exercises that measure how much of an autonomous AI attack a company's existing SOC stack actually detects. Each finding comes with tuned detections and a score the board can track.
- **Buyer:** CISO or head of detection engineering at enterprises with a SOC (roughly 1,000–10,000 employees).
- **Buyers × ACV (estimate):** About 15–20K enterprises with a SOC worldwide. Pricing is $80–150K per year.
  - $10M ARR ≈ 90 customers.
  - $100M ARR ≈ 900 customers, about 5%.
- **Why now:** Corma's own study found AI attackers succeeded 88% of the time while AI defenders detected 12% (cited by Fortune and Pulse2). Boards are asking whether the company could detect an attack by an AI agent.
- **Funded first mover and room for a second:** Corma is building the defender *model* and has said nothing about selling a test. Nobody owns an independent *measurement* of AI-attacker detection. **But** the breach-and-attack-simulation (BAS) vendors are adjacent: AttackIQ, SafeBreach, Cymulate and Picus. So are the autonomous pentest vendors: Pentera, Horizon3, XBOW and RunSybil. Any of them can add an "AI attacker" mode. The room is narrow.
- **30–90 day pilot:** 30 days in a staging tenant connected to the customer's EDR and SIEM, replaying 50 AI-attacker campaigns. Pass if detection coverage is under 50% and at least 10 detections are tuned and adopted.
- **Moat:** A library of AI-attacker campaigns, plus detection results benchmarked across customers (a cross-customer dataset).
- **Kill risk:** It becomes a feature of the BAS vendors. Frontier labs give away red-team tooling. CISOs file it under "another BAS" with no new budget line.
- **Scores:** Pain 7 · Urgency 7 · ROI clarity 6 · Customer accessibility 6 · Pilot speed 8 · Market size 7 · Expansion 6 · Venture potential 7 · Defensibility 5 · Why now 8 · Competition position 4 → **avg 6.5**

### #3. Agent-native workspace for client-facing service firms (an angle on Ando)

- **Pitch:** A shared client workspace (replacing Slack Connect and email) where an agency or consultancy, its client and both sides' AI agents work in one place, with each agent's scope, approvals and billing tracked.
- **Buyer:** COO or head of delivery at agencies, consultancies and managed-service firms with 50–2,000 staff.
- **Buyers × ACV (estimate):** About 20–30K such firms in the US and EU. Pricing is $30–80K per year, which falls below the $50K bar for smaller firms.
  - $10M ARR ≈ 200 firms.
  - $100M ARR ≈ 2,000 firms, about 8%.
- **Why now:** Agents now deliver part of the client work. Service firms have no neutral place where client-side and firm-side agents work together, and no way to show which work an agent did for billing.
- **Funded first mover and room for a second:** Ando (Accel, Index, Emergence) is building a horizontal internal Slack replacement. Viktor (an AI coworker inside Slack, $75M) attacks the same problem from inside Slack. The room is the cross-company, client-facing segment. **Why there may be no room:** this is the "destination subsidy" problem from STATUS.md. Slack and Teams give agent features away with seats, and a change of messaging tool rarely starts with service firms. It overlaps the killed thesis H (agent approvals).
- **30–90 day pilot:** One agency moves 3 client accounts onto the workspace for 45 days. Pass if both sides use it each week and agent-delivered work is billed through it.
- **Moat:** Network effects across client and firm organizations (each client brings other firms). The agent-work records become billing evidence.
- **Kill risk:** Slack Connect plus Agentforce ships the same thing. Sales cycles are long because both sides must say yes. ACV comes in under $50K.
- **Scores:** Pain 5 · Urgency 5 · ROI clarity 5 · Customer accessibility 7 · Pilot speed 7 · Market size 7 · Expansion 7 · Venture potential 7 · Defensibility 5 · Why now 7 · Competition position 4 → **avg 6.0**

## 4. Verdict

- **No forming category from this window clears the bar.** The best is the AI-attacker detection range (6.5), followed by the shift engineer for process plants (6.2).
- **Method lesson:** studying top-tier rounds confirms where capital is going, but by design it finds categories where a top-tier firm has already crowned a leader. In 21 of the 24 core rounds, the category already had several funded players. The three uncontested categories (industrial agent OS, agent-native messaging, defensive frontier model) are uncontested either because incumbents loom over them or because entry needs a lab's level of capital.
- **If the founders pursue one of these:** the process-plant shift engineer is the only one where founder access to buyers (plant operators) could change the scores. It is worth 10 discovery calls with plant managers before any deep dive.
