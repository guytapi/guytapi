## 1. Role 1: First-principles product thinker

**Extreme state (2028-29):** a mid-size B2B vendor (300 engineers, 2,000 customers) ships 300-1,000 behavior changes a week. Most are written by agents, many are behind per-tenant flags, and some are generated per customer (custom fields, workflows, AI prompt tweaks). On the other side, each enterprise customer runs 10-100 agents that call the vendor through API, MCP and even UI. Nobody reads changelogs anymore. The consumer of a change is a machine.

**Second-order consequences**
1. The unit of "change" stops being the release and becomes the behavior delta per tenant. A change that is harmless for 1,990 customers breaks 10. Which 10 cannot be known from the diff, only from each customer's actual usage.
2. "Breaking" becomes semantic, not schema. Defaults, ordering, rate limits, permission semantics, AI-feature output distributions and timing all change. Spec diffing (oasdiff, oxpecker) misses most of these.
3. Customer agents fail silently. They don't open tickets. They produce wrong downstream actions, and the failure surfaces weeks later as churn risk, a disputed invoice or an incident at the customer.
4. Enterprise procurement responds by demanding change consent: a freeze window, per-tenant pinning, notice for material behavior changes and termination rights. DORA-style regimes already require "material development" notice for FS.

**Third-order consequences**
5. The vendor's release velocity becomes capped by its slowest, most conservative enterprise customer, unless the vendor can prove per customer that a change is safe for that customer. **Compatibility evidence becomes the throttle on agent-speed shipping.** Vendors with per-tenant compatibility proof ship 10x faster to enterprise than vendors without it.
6. Version and behavior pinning sprawl: each tenant runs on a different behavior vector. Vendors need Stripe-like versioning for behavior, not just API shape.
7. Customers' agents start negotiating stability: "pin me to behavior set B until my eval passes."

**Candidate infrastructure layers**
- **L1. Tenant Compatibility Gate ("Hammer-as-a-service"):** capture each tenant's real usage (API/MCP traces, workflow configs, agent call patterns) plus any customer-declared expectations as executable *behavior pins*. Replay every candidate change against every tenant's pins in shadow. Emit a per-tenant verdict (unaffected / changed-benign / changed-material / broken) and gate rollout per tenant.
- **L2. Change-consent and behavior-pinning ledger:** per-tenant behavior versioning plus consent workflow. Material changes need tenant acceptance or auto-pin, with a signed record for contract and DORA purposes.
- **L3. Tenant-scoped signed change feed for customer agents:** a vendor-issued, machine-readable "what changed for *you*" feed, consumed by the customer's agents before they act.

L3 alone collides with releases.sh, oxpecker, mendapi and the YC RFS on the consumer side. L2 alone is a feature of a feature-flag platform. **L1 is the deep layer.** It produces the evidence that L2 and L3 need. L3 is the distribution, and L2 is the monetizable enterprise promise.

## 2. Role 2: Technical infrastructure architect

**What breaks at 100x:**
- *Semver and changelogs* assume a human reads release notes. They break when there are 1,000 deltas a week and the readers are agents.
- *Feature flags* (LaunchDarkly, Statsig) segment **exposure**, but they don't know **impact**. They tell you who got the flag, not whose workflow behaved differently.
- *Replay tools* (Speedscale, Keploy) diff behavior per service in CI with sampled traffic. They aren't tenant-partitioned, they don't keep a per-tenant baseline over months, and they have no customer-facing contract semantics.
- *Contract testing* (Pact/PactFlow) needs the consumer to write and publish contracts. External customers never do it, and their agents certainly won't.
- *Codecov Impact Analysis* knows hot code paths, not which customer is on them.

**Missing primitive: the tenant behavior surface.** This is a continuously maintained, per-tenant set of observed request→response behaviors (including MCP tool calls and AI-feature outputs within tolerance bands). It is keyed by tenant, normalized and de-identified, and versioned. It supports one operation: `impact(change_candidate) → {tenant: delta_class, evidence}`.

Analogy: OpenTelemetry standardized *what happened*. This standardizes *what each customer depends on*. Salesforce Hammer (210M customer tests) proves the operation is valuable and buildable at one company. The opportunity is to make it a product for the other ~5,000 B2B vendors.

**Hard parts:** these are a moat if solved and death if not.
1. Deterministic replay of stateful multi-tenant SaaS: side effects, time, randomness and LLM nondeterminism. AI features need distributional comparison, not equality.
2. Data handling. Per-tenant traces are customer data. They must be captured and replayed inside the vendor's VPC, with BYOC deployment.
3. Noise. Classifying "changed-benign" vs "changed-material" per tenant without drowning engineers.

**Could a platform team build it in a sprint?** No, not the per-tenant baseline, shadow replay and classification at scale. But the top 1% of vendors (Salesforce, Stripe, Shopify) already built their own. That caps the top of the market and validates the middle.

## 3. Role 3: Skeptical VC partner

**Where the money is:** vendor-side only. Consumer-side change detection (oxpecker, mendapi, YC RFS) is a cheap developer tool with low ACV and a free tier from releases.sh. On the vendor side, the economic argument is NRR and enterprise velocity. If an L1 gate lets a vendor keep shipping at agent speed **and** offer enterprises a "no unconsented behavior change" guarantee, that is sold by the VP Eng and the CRO together.

**Who captures value:** the most likely winner is an existing release-safety or flag platform adding tenant-scoped impact: LaunchDarkly (per-context targeting already), Datadog Bits Release (telemetry plus PR plans), Harness, or Speedscale moving up. That is the core risk. Each holds 50-70% of the needed data.

**What would make me lead the seed:**
- 3 design partners (B2B SaaS, 200+ enterprise tenants, agent-heavy shipping) where a back-test on 90 days of deploys shows the gate would have flagged ≥60% of change-caused enterprise escalations *by tenant* before rollout, at under 5% false-material rate.
- A CRO or CS VP co-signing the budget, because "change consent" closed or saved a named enterprise deal.
- Evidence that customer agents (not humans) are a growing share of API/MCP calls at those partners, which makes silent breakage the dominant failure.

**What stops me:**
- Pain is visible inside companies but there is no public proof of rising change-caused tickets (the brief says not to stop on that, but it lowers urgency and ROI clarity).
- Replay of stateful SaaS is notoriously hard. Diffy (Twitter) and many shadow-traffic tools stayed niche.
- Customers' agents are mostly pre-scale in 2026. The MCP drift data shows churn is concentrated in a few servers (dev.to follow-up).
- Incumbent adjacency: LaunchDarkly plus Datadog plus Speedscale could converge on this within 12 months once "tenant impact" becomes a buzzword.

**Red-team verdict:** the deep layer (tenant-scoped pre-rollout impact plus gating plus consent record) is open in public sources. But:
1. Its build difficulty is the same thing that has kept shadow-replay niche for a decade.
2. The closest incumbents (Datadog, LaunchDarkly, Speedscale) each own a piece and the distribution.
3. Pain and urgency are inferred, not evidenced. The customer-agent-breakage driver is real at the MCP layer but early and concentrated.
4. The L2 "consent record" piece risks the round-28 lesson that a neutral record isn't where the money goes. It survives only as a vendor-owned release control, not as a neutral record.

## 5. Finalist write-up: **Tenant Compatibility Gate** ("Hammer for every B2B vendor")

- **One-line problem:** B2B vendors shipping hundreds of agent-written changes a week cannot know which specific customers (and customers' agents) will see behavior change, so they either surprise enterprises or slow down to the most conservative customer's pace.
- **Why now:** AI-attributed commits went from ~2M to ~37M/month in public GitHub repos in H1 2026 (snippet via Harness/Datadog-adjacent result, unverified). There are 917 public breaking changes a year across just 30 vendors (oxpecker). 4.4% of MCP servers changed tool contracts within 36 hours. Customer agents are becoming API/MCP consumers that fail silently. Enterprises are writing material-change notice and adverse-change termination rights into AI contracts, and DORA requires notice of material developments. YC's Fall 2026 RFS validates the consumer side, which pushes the opening one layer deeper to the vendor side.
- **Exact buyer:** VP Engineering / Head of Platform (budget), co-sponsored by CRO or VP Customer Success (enterprise retention).
- **Exact ICP:** B2B SaaS with public API and/or MCP server, 150-1,500 engineers, ≥200 enterprise tenants, ≥50% of PRs agent-authored, daily deploys, enterprise contracts with change-notice clauses (FS/health customers under DORA-style obligations a plus). Examples of the shape: vertical SaaS, fintech infra, devtools with enterprise tiers. No names validated.
- **Current workaround:** feature flags plus slow ring rollouts. "Enterprise tier on a lag" release trains. Manual CSM heads-up emails. Changelog plus 30-day notice. Engineers grep logs for "who calls this endpoint". Top vendors build in-house (Salesforce Hammer, Stripe versioning, Cadwyn-style OSS).
- **Why incumbents can't easily own it:** flag platforms know exposure, not behavior. Observability vendors know health, not per-tenant semantic baselines. Replay vendors are per service and CI-scoped. The product needs long-lived per-tenant behavior baselines kept in the vendor's VPC, plus a customer-facing contract surface (consent and feed), which none of their motions sell. **Honest caveat:** Datadog and LaunchDarkly could each get 60% there in 2-3 quarters.
- **30-day MVP:** a BYOC collector (API gateway / OTel / MCP server middleware) that builds per-tenant endpoint/tool usage fingerprints, plus a PR bot. For each PR, it runs a static diff and targeted shadow replay of the top-N tenant fingerprints that touch changed paths. It comments "affects 14 tenants: 3 material (Acme: default sort changed on /invoices list, used by their agent nightly)". It writes a per-tenant change note to a JSON feed endpoint. No gating yet.
- **Pilot design:** 2-3 vendors. Back-test 90 days of deploys against tagged enterprise escalations and support tickets. Then run live 30 days in comment-only mode. Success means ≥60% of change-caused enterprise escalations attributed to the right tenant pre-merge, <5% false-material, and ≥1 rollout actually held for a tenant by the eng team.
- **Pricing hypothesis:** platform fee plus per-tenant baseline, e.g. $3-8 per active tenant per month with a $60K-250K ACV for ICP. An enterprise "change-consent" add-on (per-tenant pinning plus signed consent log) lets vendors upsell to their own enterprise customers.
- **Expansion path:** comment bot → per-tenant rollout gating (integrate with LaunchDarkly/Statsig/Argo) → behavior pinning and consent workflows → tenant-scoped signed change feed consumed by customers' agents (the "change intelligence" layer, with vendor distribution) → cross-vendor compatibility network where customers' agents subscribe to every vendor's feed.
- **Moat:** per-tenant behavior baselines accumulated over months, which are hard to recreate. Replay determinism tooling for stateful SaaS and AI features. If L3 takes off, a two-sided network of vendors publishing and customer agents subscribing.
- **Why $10B+:** if B2B behavior changes continuously and customers are agents, every vendor needs per-tenant compatibility proof to sell to enterprises. That is the "Stripe versioning plus Salesforce Hammer" capability for the whole SaaS economy, plus a change network between vendors and customer agents. Plausible only if the customer-agent integration wave reaches enterprise scale by 2028.
- **Competitors and adjacent threats:** Datadog Bits Release, Harness, LaunchDarkly/Statsig, Speedscale, Keploy, PactFlow, Codecov; on the consumer side oxpecker, mendapi, releases.sh and the YC RFS cohort; MCP gateways (Gravitee, Runlayer); in-house builds at top vendors.
- **One sentence to a CTO:** "Before each deploy, we tell you exactly which of your enterprise customers (and their agents) will see different behavior, and hold the rollout just for them, so you can ship at agent speed without surprising anyone."
- **Hard kill criteria:**
  1. The back-test at 2 of 3 design partners attributes <40% of change-caused escalations to the correct tenant pre-merge.
  2. False-material rate >15% after tuning, so engineers mute the bot.
  3. Design partners say per-tenant replay data cannot leave their VPC *and* won't run BYOC.
  4. No CRO/CS co-sponsor emerges, meaning the budget is eng-tooling only and capped at <$50K.
  5. Datadog or LaunchDarkly ships tenant-scoped impact analysis before the first paid pilot.
  6. Customer-agent share of API/MCP traffic at partners is <5% with no growth.

**Classification: weak B (not A).** The vendor-side, tenant-scoped layer looks open and has a clear analog (Salesforce Hammer) proving the operation is valuable. The pain it rests on (customer agents silently breaking on vendor change, enterprises demanding change consent) is starting now but isn't yet evidenced at budget level. Estimated ~10-15% odds that a data back-test passes. It fails A on urgency, ROI clarity and pilot speed (replay of stateful SaaS is slow to make reliable).

**Precise 14-day test (data, not interviews):** pick 2 B2B SaaS companies with ≥200 enterprise tenants. Export 90 days of deploy/PR history, API/MCP gateway logs keyed by tenant, and support/escalation tickets. Join ticket timestamps and tenant to deploys touching endpoints that tenant used in the prior 14 days. **Pass** if ≥20% of enterprise escalations correlate with a specific tenant-touching change *and* a static-plus-fingerprint heuristic would have named the tenant pre-merge in ≥50% of those cases. **Fail** if fewer than 10% of escalations are change-caused, because then H6 is a comms problem, which is the decoy.
